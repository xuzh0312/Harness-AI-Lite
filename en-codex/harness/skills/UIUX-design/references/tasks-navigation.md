# Tasks, Navigation, and Walkthroughs

**When to use this reference.** Use it when discussing feature scope, when users struggle to find an entry point, or when preparing to test a prototype's usability.

**Start with one actual experience.** Ask where the user was the last time the issue came up, what they had available, and what they did in the end. Capture the trigger, current approach, obstacle, and outcome. Ask when users would need a requested reminder to learn whether they need a timed notification, a way to resume work, or a way to find information. Distinguish facts reported by the user, observed behavior, and hypotheses still to test.

**Map enough of the task to make a decision.** Write “entry point → information needed to decide → primary action → recognizable result.” Add a recovery path for a likely mistake. For each proposed feature, ask which step the user would be unable to complete without it. Let the user decide how to handle that gap. Keep an existing workflow that already serves the goal.

**Organize entry points the way users look for things.** Use the user's own words to identify whether they are looking for a known item, browsing a category, or following a sequence. Choose an understandable basis for grouping items at the same level. If users keep hesitating between two entry points, show them a few real items to group. Observe where they put the items and what names they give the groups.

**Verify with a task.** Give only a concrete goal, such as finding the question they answered incorrectly yesterday. Observe where they go first, where they pause, and whether they recover on their own. Avoid naming the target control. To check discoverability, let someone who has not seen the steps try first, then compare the outcome with the acceptance scenario. Record a walkthrough by someone who has seen the steps as guided. Treat simulated walkthrough conclusions as hypotheses. In the handoff, describe the most important obstacle with a reproducible scenario.
